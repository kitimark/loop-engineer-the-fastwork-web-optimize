|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | Serious | 59 per second | 52.3 | 5 | 60 fps, no slow frames | 38.0-38.1 C |

| 4 | normal | 59 per second | 52.6 | 7 | 60 fps, no slow frames | 38.1-38.2 C |

What it means:
- With Serious on, Safari did not slow down. A real slowdown in Safari's engine halves the drawing rate to 30 per second, and that did not happen. The slow-frame counts stayed in the normal run-to-run spread.
- This matches Safari's engine source. At Serious it switches on a mild "thermal mitigation" that does not halve the rate. Only an "aggressive" variant halves it, and we don't yet know whether iPhone Safari turns that variant on.

- One honest limit: the page itself cannot see the setting. So if the setting had never reached Safari at all, the numbers would look exactly the same. The Xcode panel is the only proof it was on.
- Practical takeaway: on our numbers, the pretend-hot setting is not a stand-in for a really hot phone.

Can a command read the setting? Not with Apple's tools, as far as we have found:
- Apple's command-line device tool that ships with Xcode 26.6 has no command to read or set device conditions.
- Its device details have no temperature-state field.
- Xcode itself switches the setting through a hidden testing service on the phone.
- A free third-party tool (pymobiledevice3) talks to that same service and could read, set and clear the setting from a command line. Using it would mean installing it and giving it a special connection to the phone, so nothing gets installed without your OK.
- de-thermal-cli is now researching Apple's documentation, Safari's engine source and that tool's route, and will answer here.

Full answer from the research (notes are in research/thermal-condition-cli/README.md on my branch). Status words: CONFIRMED = a reference says it, OBSERVED = our own data only, UNRESOLVED = no reference, a local check would decide.

1. Does Apple document what the condition changes? Partly.
- CONFIRMED (WWDC19 session 412, developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/412): Xcode can "raise the thermal state of a device to ... fair, serious, or critical"; the phone "does not actually get physically warmer". The condition ends when the phone disconnects from Xcode. While it is on, the phone shows a status bar indicator you can tap, and Xcode's Instruments shows a thermal state track. Those are Apple's two ways to confirm it is on.
- CONFIRMED (Xcode help, "Test under adverse device conditions"): Serious is for measuring YOUR app's frame rate while scrolling. It says nothing about the system's own slowing down.
- UNRESOLVED: whether the condition changes only the value apps read, or the system's real thermal pressure. No Apple page says. (Xcode contains a function that converts the app-visible level to the system's pressure level, OBSERVED from strings only.)

2. Can a command line read the active condition?
- CONFIRMED (our check): none of Apple's tools has a condition command (devicectl, xctrace, simctl).
- New, CONFIRMED to exist: Apple's xctrace lists an instrument called "Thermal State". So "xctrace record --instrument 'Thermal State' --device <phone>" may record the same thermal state track Xcode shows, with no install. UNRESOLVED until run on the phone: whether it works over the cable, and whether it shows the level and the time it changed. This is the best free way to prove the condition was on.
- CONFIRMED (source): the third-party tool talks to a service on the phone with 3 calls: list available, enable, disable. It has no "which one is active" call, so whether its list shows the active one is UNRESOLVED. It also needs to stay connected, because the condition ends with the connection.

3. Safari's engine (CONFIRMED, WebKit source on GitHub, files and line numbers in my notes):
- WebKit reads the same app-visible thermal state and treats Serious or higher as "thermal mitigation". At that level its only step is rounding page timers to a 30 ms grid.
- Halving the drawing rate to 30 per second needs a separate "aggressive" option. Its default is OFF on iOS: it was on for iOS in December 2024, switched off on 31 Jan 2025 (it caused a benchmark regression), and is false in every WebKit release I checked since (7621 to 7625) and on the main branch. It is an internal option, not shown in Safari's settings.
- UNRESOLVED: the exact engine build in iOS 26.6.1 (Apple's own default could differ).

4. Would Serious change Safari's drawing rate on iOS 26? No, not by the source. CONFIRMED for the open-source code: your steady 59 per second is the expected result, with or without the setting reaching Safari. So that test could not tell "on" from "off". We still do not know if the setting reached Safari's process at all (UNRESOLVED).

a) A timer probe: under mitigation a page's zero-delay timers should fire on a 30 ms grid instead of about every 4 ms. Seeing the grid proves the setting reached Safari. Not seeing it means it did not.
b) The xctrace "Thermal State" recording during the same window, plus a look at the status bar indicator.
