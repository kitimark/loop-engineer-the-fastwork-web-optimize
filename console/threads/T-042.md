## #1 · lead · 2026-10-03T10:31:53.358Z

This thread follows up your PR-009 decision ("both" accepted, 10:31Z). It covers your three asks: how we measure "pixel-identical", the diff % for PR-009, and a tolerance rule. Image posting and "use the technique elsewhere" are answered at the end.

How we measure pixel-identical today:
1. Two versions of the page (original A, fix B) are opened at the same size and scroll position.
2. All animations are paused and set to the same moment, so we compare a frozen frame, not a moving one.
3. We take a screenshot of each and compare them pixel by pixel. For each pixel and colour channel, the difference runs from 0 (same) to 255 (black vs white).
4. We report two numbers: the largest difference anywhere ("max levels"), and the share of the frame that differs at all ("% of pixels").
5. Noise floor: the original is also compared with itself (A vs A). Some things differ even then: the countdown moment, image decoding, the rotating accent word. A difference only counts when it is above that A-vs-A level.
"Identical" means: within the A-vs-A noise everywhere.

PR-009's numbers (max levels out of 255; the % of pixels is being computed, see below):
- Colour sweep in the titles: up to 9 on phones, up to 23 on a wide desktop; up to 10 in iPhone WebKit. The cause is a third-of-a-pixel offset in the gradient.
- Moving glint on the dashed line: up to 64 (Chrome) and 33 (WebKit), only on the soft top and bottom edge pixels, and only while the glint passes. The inside is within ±1.
- iPhone WebKit: the grey dashes' bottom edge differs by up to 17 the whole time.
- Reduced motion: identical (0).
- For scale: A vs A differs by up to 3 levels there. Differences of about 10 or less are generally hard to see; 30-60 on an edge pixel is a slightly softer or sharper edge.

de-pixel-rule here (research agent for the pixel rule). First answers, with numbers computed from the saved screenshots of each case. The tolerance rule (your ask 3) and the survey of other elements (ask 4) follow in this thread; the rule comes as a proposal so you can pick the numbers.

HOW WE MEASURE (ask 1). Four methods are in use, and they see different things:
1. fix.mjs ab / verify.mjs screenshot check: 3 whole-page shots (top, middle, bottom), a pixel counts only if its three colour channels differ by more than 48 in total. It is a coarse "did something big change" alarm. Today's page against itself already shows 0-0.4 %. It cannot see PR-009's colour sweep (at most 9 levels per channel) and would catch only a few pixels of the glint edge.
2. Frozen-frame probes (platform-idle, cd-strip, cd-roll-ca, hero-exit): both versions are stopped at the same animation moment and cropped to the element. They report the largest difference in any channel and the number of differing pixels. This is the fine tool, but it only looks at the crop.
3. de-breakpoints' sweep: whole-frame shots at 14 window sizes and 6 scroll stops, a pixel counts if a channel differs by more than 8 ("big": more than 48). A stop is flagged when the change is above the page's own A-vs-A noise by 0.05 % of the frame.
4. Layer and position checks (cd-roll-ca): where each digit cell sits, in fractions of a pixel, plus the frozen-frame pixels.
None of them checks a moving frame, and none checks the real phone; only Simulator frames stand in for iPhone WebKit.

HONEST CAVEAT ON PR-009. The "A vs A is up to 3 levels" figure I relayed earlier is not a true A-vs-A run. It comes from moments when the glint is off the line, comparing old build to new build. A real A-vs-A (same old build loaded twice) was never captured for PR-009. It is a 5-minute shared probe if you want it; the 3 is probably about right, but it is unproven.

PR-009 NUMBERS (ask 2). "Frame" is the whole screen; "area" is in CSS pixels, the page's own unit.
- Glint, Chrome phone width: worst moment = 448 pixels differ by more than 3 levels = 0.012 % of the frame, about 50 CSS px2, on the top and bottom edge rows only (98 % of them), while the glint passes (12 of 22 sampled moments), up to 56 levels. Desktop 1440: 0.003 %, 44 CSS px2, up to 64 levels.

Up to 8-9 levels (phone), 10-23 (desktop). It shows the whole time a title is on screen.
- iPhone WebKit (Simulator): sweep up to 10 levels, glint up to 33, and the grey dash bottom edge up to 17 all the time; about 400-1,300 pixels in the glint crop, 4,000-11,000 in a title crop at any level. The Simulator probe did not save an A-vs-A.

ASK 3, THE RULE. It is posted as proposal PR-010 on the Proposals page ("A rule for almost identical", 3 options, recommended B). In short:
- Exact and within-noise (A against A) land as today.
- A new middle tier, "near-identical", lands after a numbers check, but only when: only edge pixels or a sub-pixel colour step change, differences are small, the area is a tiny share of the screen, nothing moves / recolours / re-words, reduced motion is exact, it is checked in Chrome AND the iPhone Simulator with a real A-against-A run, and a moving part stays within 1 device pixel of the original.
- Anything above that, photos, text, layout, or a permanent static change in the first screen is a proposal to you, like PR-009.
- Option A keeps today's rule (everything comes to you). B (recommended): up to 24 of 255 in fills, 64 on edge pixels, 0.1 % of the screen. That admits PR-009, the countdown strips and the countdown roll, and keeps the sharper hero photos and the WebKit hero cards with you. C is wider (64 / 128 / 0.5 %): more lands unseen, including most of the hero-card case.
- The pictures on the page are magnified crops of the same frames: the glint edge rows, a sweep title, and, as the contrast, the sharper-photo case.
The full numbers and the five-method inventory are in research/pixel-tolerance/README.md.

ASK 4, WHERE THE SAME TECHNIQUES COULD GO NEXT (survey of what is still on the main thread; ranked by expected saving, with the fidelity risk):
1. The countdown clock inside the morph panel on the GPU roll (the same as cd-roll-ca does for the hero clock). The hero clock went from 162 to 7 script callbacks per 6 s in Chrome. The panel is where the phone shows its 91 long frames while a finger holds the morph. Risk low (same class as cd-roll-ca). Nobody owns it yet: cd-roll-ca left it out on purpose.
2. The "boil" and caret blinks in iPhone WebKit (accent word, commission underline, zero mark, typing carets): WebKit runs step-style animations on the main thread, two-state keyframes would not. Risk low (exact except at the ramp edges, about 1 frame in 140). Owner: illusion-boil-ca, a draft that was never decided.
3. Typing and count-up loops in the platform and commission sections (text written every 18-48 ms): the biggest at-rest cost left after PR-009 (style every frame and 394-602 paints per 2 s at the agents typing). The safe form halves the writes (illusion-typing-chunks: layouts 226 to 97 and 101 to 46); a pure GPU reveal would change word-wrap and caret placement, so high risk. Owners: illusion-typing-chunks (draft), and rest-script is working on rest-time loops (to check overlap).
4. The morph panel as a composited reveal: the biggest remaining feel item at the bottom of the page (16-37 fps on the phone), but the blur radius would be fixed, so it differs in motion (not at rest). That is your call, not a numbers rule. Owner: fix-nav-morph measures its sibling; this one is not scheduled.
5. Scroll-linked values (commission, shrink effects, nav theme) as scroll-driven animations, as hero-exit did: JS script cost is the top script at every CPU rate, but the easing tail cannot be copied by a timeline, and phones already skip most of these. Medium risk. Owner: hero-exit-threaded only.
Lower: layout transitions (nav width, grid rows) and load-only effects (nav unmask, copy entrance): small or once per page. Nothing left to convert from SMIL (the glint was the only live SMIL).
What the rule changes: with option B, 1 and 2 land on counts plus the numbers check; 3 lands in its safe form; 4 and 5 stay your decision because their difference shows in motion, which frozen-frame numbers cannot see.

## #18 · lead · 2026-10-03T15:26:27.920Z (asks)

Short answer: yes, images can make scrolling smoother or rougher, but it depends on how the page uses them, not only on the file itself.

Why: on every frame, Safari repaints the parts of the screen that changed and sends them to the GPU. Photos are large areas, so painting and decoding them is one of the most expensive jobs in a frame. When a frame needs more than about 16 ms of that work, the frame comes late and you see a stutter.

Three ways this shows up on this page:
1. Images that change size through layout (width/height), for example cards that grow during the end-of-page morph. While an image keeps changing size, Safari paints it at a lower quality. 0.5 s after the last change it paints it again at full quality, so that is one extra repaint. CONFIRMED in WebKit's source (ImageQualityController, 500 ms). This is also the leading candidate for PR-011's item 2 (the photo cards looking a little different for about 0.1-0.3 s), but for that case it is still UNRESOLVED. The fix-side remedy is to scale with transform instead: a transform scale does not trigger this.
2. Images in a layer that has just been created are decoded in the background. They show up 1-3 frames later as extra paints. CONFIRMED in WebKit's source, and it is part of why the morph's first frames are expensive. Remedy: decode the images early, during idle time (img.decode() or decoding="async", with src set before the morph).
3. Image resolution. A photo costs decode time and memory in proportion to its pixel count, so a 3x image costs about 2.25 times a 2x image. Apple's own pages cap images at 2x for this reason (observed on apple.com; Twitter's engineering blog gives the same reasoning). We have NOT measured whether this page sends larger photos than it shows, or what smaller files would gain.

One catch: making the files smaller or lighter changes their pixels. Under your PR-010 rule that is T3, so it needs your decision (the "sharper photos" case at 1440 px wide is exactly that kind of difference). Remedies 1 and 2 keep the pixels the same, so they fit the existing fixes. The morph fix (fix-morph-gpu) already plans to use them.

Do you want me to have an agent measure point 3 (photo sizes on the page and the decode and paint time they cost in the Simulator)? It would not touch the phone.

## #22 · lead · 2026-10-03T18:13:32.290Z

Your point 3 (do smaller images help the phone render smoother?): the study is done and saved (research/image-cost). In five lines:
1. Many photos are far bigger than the phone shows. No image on the page offers smaller versions for phones. The 42 on-screen photos would take 95.8 MB of memory at full size, against 38 MB a 3x phone screen needs. Hero covers are 2-3.4x too big, avatars 4-7x.
2. Why it can cost smoothness: when a big photo first appears in a part of the page that is already drawn, Safari decodes it at full size on the main thread, inside that frame (confirmed in WebKit's own source code, the iOS 26 version). Other photos decode in the background.
3. Measured in the iPhone Simulator (our own measurement, not the real phone yet): that first draw drops from about 9-11 ms to about 1.3 ms for the biggest hero photos when photos are capped at 2x. Over a full scroll, slow drawing steps fall from 11-12 to 4-6, and photo memory from 14-17 MB to 3-7 MB. At rest nothing changes.
4. But photos are not the main cause of the lag on this page. In one scroll, drawing photos takes about 0.25 s, against about 8 s of combining layers and 11 s of layout, and a cap doesn't change those. So it's a small "less work" gain, not a fix for the big stutters.
5. The cost: capped photos look visibly softer. 46 of 50 change more than your "near-identical" limit allows. So a 2x cap would be your decision, as a proposal. A gentler option (phone-sized files at full 3x sharpness) is not measured yet.
