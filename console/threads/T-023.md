## #3 · lead · 2026-10-02T12:11:10.306Z (asks)

The research is in. It's desk work: WebKit source, Apple docs and the man pages, with nothing run.

Short answer: Safari on this Mac can't stand in for the iPhone on smoothness or timing. It can help with one narrower job.

Why not timing:
- Safari has no CPU slow-down setting, so we can't make the Mac's speed match the phone's.
- Scrolling on the Mac runs through different code (the wheel and trackpad path) than the iPhone's touch scrolling.
- The Mac has no touch, no collapsing toolbar and no phone viewport.
- Safari on the Mac is on version 26.2; the phone is on 26.6.1.
So Mac numbers wouldn't tell us how the phone feels.

What it can do: show which parts of the page WebKit recalculates, lays out and repaints for each change, and catch a fix that breaks in WebKit. Safari on the Mac and on the iPhone share that part of the engine (confirmed in the WebKit source).

Two ways to use it:
- A) Real Safari, driven by Apple's safaridriver. It needs one step from you: run `sudo safaridriver --enable` once, which asks for your admin password. A Safari window would open on screen during each check. It uses a separate test profile, so your own Safari history and logins aren't touched, and it can't use the camera.
- B) The WebKit build already on this Mac from Playwright (a browser-testing tool). It needs nothing from you and opens no window. It can pretend to be a 430-wide screen at the iPhone's pixel density and record WebKit's timeline. It's a test build, not Safari itself, so how closely it matches is still open.

My recommendation: try B as a small experiment for those "what did WebKit recalculate" counts only, next to the iPhone Simulator we already use, and keep all timing on the real phone. I'd skip A unless B falls short. The Simulator is also being fixed so it doesn't restart from scratch for every check.
