Sixteen Milliseconds: Loop Engineering the fastwork.com Scroll

My first AI loop engineering, built from scratch: ten days, one iPhone and a team of AI agents optimizing how fastwork.com scrolls, until a stuttering page felt smooth under my thumb.

Part one · Told by me, drafted by Claude from my notes and the log

A personal study of the public page at fastwork.com, done on a local copy. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Fastwork. The clips are screen recordings of that local copy, made for this study only.

The optimized page and the source code are not public yet. I am asking Fastwork for permission, and I will publish the link and the code only after they approve.

Every fix, measurement and finger test aimed at one setup: Safari on my iPhone 15 Pro Max. Times are UTC.

Before day one

It started with a headline, on the evening of 28 September 2026. I was sitting in Milk cafe when I read it: Fastwork, the Thai freelance marketplace, was shutting down.

It wasn't. The news was a marketing move for a rebrand, the move from fastwork.co to a global platform at fastwork.com. I guessed what was coming, so I opened fastwork.com to see it for myself.

The new site uses morph design: shapes that melt into each other as you scroll, cards that fade and fold, a menu that grows out of a button. My first wow came from the headline at the top, the “Show the world what you can do” animation.

That animation was quite smooth. Then I did my first slow scroll to see the other animations. The hero cards moved out so laggy. So did everything after them: “People still need people”, the user carousel, all the way to the end of the page. On my iPhone 15 Pro Max it was so laggy.

That is what made me want to fix it. I wanted to see how amazing this website would be with smooth animation on my iPhone. And I wanted to know something bigger: if I build an AI loop and give it a goal and a few clues, can AI get there on its own?

A word about the people who made this website. This is not a criticism of fastwork.com. A team launching a new global platform has to make it work first and make it best later, and the morph design is ambitious work that I admire. What follows is my own experiment, done for education. I hope this article helps the Fastwork team with the rendering performance, and helps the platform reach the world-class standard Fastwork is aiming for.

How I measure

  • My iPhone shows a new picture every 16.7 ms. For each picture, the page has to finish its own work in time.
  • A slow frame took the page over 34 ms, so at least one picture is lost. A very slow frame took over 50 ms. My goal is no very slow frames. Slow frames are the count I tracked most.

Day 1 · Wed 30 Sep: One complaint, one goal

I'm a software engineer, mostly back end. I knew a few web animation optimization tricks, but I have a full-time job, so I had no time to fix a page like this by hand. So I asked a different question: if I build an AI loop, give it a goal and a few clues, and let the agents investigate, measure, probe and fix by themselves, can AI get there on its own? That was the experiment, my first try at loop engineering, built from scratch. The AI team had to investigate and optimize the site’s compiled source, the files fastwork.com actually ships, without rewriting the page from scratch. I didn’t care about code quality, only the rendering result: could it get SOOO ULTRA SMOOTHHHH?

It ran on a fixed budget: Claude Max, the $200 plan, on 3 to 5 accounts. In this experiment I used three, carrying the work over between them as each one’s weekly usage ran out.

I added the pieces through the evening. At 19:33, one agent per problem, each in its own copy of the code. At 20:00, tests for every tool. At 20:17, a hard rule: rebase only. At 21:14 I made the lead the orchestrator until the job was done, and at 21:21 I set the goal in one line:

fix animation until the website renders SOOOOO ULTRA SMOOTH

The capitals are a joke. “ULTRA SMOOTHHHHHH” is a viral food and marketing trend in Thailand: an Italian gelato with an ultra-fine, slow-melting texture, made popular by the brand Parameter.

What I decided

  • Study a local copy only; never touch the live site (18:06).
  • One agent per topic, each in its own worktree; rebase only, no merge commits (19:33, 20:17).

What I learned

The loop is very fast at the work after a good question. It gets much better when someone who knows the field asks it. My part was knowing what "smooth" feels like, refusing numbers my thumb didn't agree with, and pointing at the next place to look.

It's still hard to believe that the team could optimize this so well. I went in expecting to lose the money for three Claude Max accounts for nothing, because I believed AI couldn't do it. In the real world, a software engineer asked to optimize a site from its compiled code alone would call it impossible; rewriting it would be easier for a human. The team did it anyway, and I really appreciate that.

The experiment wasn't perfect. We made a lot of mistakes we can improve on next time, and I think we should try again, since my three subscriptions still have most of the month left. Looking back over the ten days, we could have used less time:

  • Knowing the answer's direction. If I had known from the start what the problem was, and that the GPU could solve it, I could have aimed the team at researching that. I think it would have saved about two days.
  • Measuring from my gesture. If I had given the team the problems I found and asked it to capture my own swipe first, it could have built a real measurement from it. I'm sure that would have saved a lot of time.
  • More test devices. The limit wasn't only the session usage we had to carry over every five hours. My MacBook ran one test queue, and the one real iPhone could test only one thing at a time. With more devices, agents wouldn't have to wait for tests.
  • Tokens spent waiting. Solving those limits would also save tokens. Agents queued for tests, sometimes for more than an hour, and then missed the prompt cache, so they had to read their whole session again, and we paid for it again.

Together, roughly 6 to 7 days instead of 8.7, and $3,000 to $5,500 less.

So an engineer who already knows the GPU technique and where this page lags could get here in about 2.5 to 5 days and $2,200 to $6,900 at list prices, against 9.4 days and $10,930 for this run. Below about 2 days nobody gets, because one iPhone can test one build at a time. By hand, I think it would take me more than a month, because the work is in the compiled files fastwork.com ships. Those are hard for a person to read, so by hand I would rather rewrite the page than fix it in place. The loop fixed it in place.
