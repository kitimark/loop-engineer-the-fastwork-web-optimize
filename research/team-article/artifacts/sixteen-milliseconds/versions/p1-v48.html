Sixteen Milliseconds

Ten days, one phone, one goal: how I built an AI loop from scratch and steered it until a stuttering page felt smooth under my thumb.

Part one · Told by me, drafted by Claude from my notes and the log

Every fix, measurement and finger test aimed at one setup: Safari on my iPhone 15 Pro Max. Times are UTC. Phone numbers marked OBSERVED come from our own checks, in our setup.

Before day one

It started with a headline, on the evening of 28 September 2026. I was sitting in Milk cafe when I read it: Fastwork, the Thai freelance marketplace, was shutting down.

It wasn't. The news was a marketing move for a rebrand, the move from fastwork.co to a global platform at fastwork.com. I guessed what was coming, so I opened fastwork.com to see it for myself.

The new site uses morph design: shapes that melt into each other as you scroll, cards that fade and fold, a menu that grows out of a button. My first wow came from the headline at the top, the “Show the world what you can do” animation.

That animation was quite smooth. Then I did my first slow scroll to see the other animations. The hero cards moved out so laggy. So did everything after them: “People still need people”, the user carousel, all the way to the end of the page. On my iPhone 15 Pro Max it was so laggy.

That is what made me want to fix it. I wanted to see how amazing this website would be with smooth animation on my iPhone.

Day 1 · Wed 30 Sep: One complaint, one goal

72 agents · 125 commits

At 17:05 UTC, just after midnight my time, I asked Claude how to connect Safari on my iPhone to the Mac. By 17:53 I had scrolled the page with the inspector attached, and I wrote my first observation in plain words: laggy at the top and at the end. Those two places stayed the targets for the whole ten days.

Three minutes later I mentioned something I had already tried on my own: Chrome on the desktop, with the CPU slowed down 4x, stuttered too. Was it the same code? That question became the plan. Copy the page to my Mac, then slow Chrome until it lags like the phone. The team calibrated it to 6x.

I'm a software engineer, mostly back end. I knew a few web animation optimization tricks, but I have a full-time job, so I had no time to fix a page like this by hand. So I asked a different question: if I build an AI loop, give it a goal and a few clues, and let the agents investigate, measure, probe and fix by themselves, can AI get there on its own? That was the experiment, my first try at loop engineering, on a fixed budget: Claude Max, the $200 plan, on 3 to 5 accounts. In this experiment I used three, carrying the work over between them as each one’s weekly usage ran out. I added the pieces through the evening. At 19:33, one agent per problem, each in its own copy of the code. At 20:00, tests for every tool. At 20:17, a hard rule: rebase only. At 21:14 I made the lead the orchestrator until the job was done, and at 21:21 I set the goal in one line:

fix animation until the website renders SOOOOO ULTRA SMOOTH

It was not smooth. At 21:49 I saw uncommitted work on main; a minute later I asked why nobody was using Chrome, and later why agents had to queue to commit. The Mac reached a load of 58. At 23:05 I told the lead it was being interrupted a lot and had to delegate. At 23:26 I asked why it needed me to spot these problems.

What I decided

  • Study a local copy only; never touch the live site (18:06).
  • One agent per topic, each in its own worktree; rebase only, no merge commits (19:33, 20:17).
  • The lead orchestrates until done, and must delegate instead of doing everything itself (21:14, 23:05).

I already knew a few ways to measure rendering, and slowing Chrome’s CPU 4x was one of them. What I wanted was the simplest way to reproduce the phone’s lag on my MacBook Pro M1, and to explain it, so I wouldn’t have to ask for a test on the real iPhone every time.

And the capitals in my goal are a joke. “ULTRA SMOOTHHHHHH” is a viral food and marketing trend in Thailand: an Italian gelato with an ultra-fine, slow-melting texture, made popular by the brand Parameter.

At 23:26 I pointed out the dirty main branch and the commit queue myself. I just wanted to prevent an issue I had run into in other AI environments: the lead agent couldn’t rebase and land work on the main branch.

Day 2 · Thu 1 Oct: "Laggy the same"

88 agents · 542 commits · 16 steps

The morning started with a warm phone. At 07:07 I reported it warm, at 74 % battery, and locked it to cool down. Then I swiped the team's first versions: less work in the countdown, the carousel and the scroll handlers. Chrome said they were better. At 07:57, version A: still laggy, like the real site. At 08:00, version B:

Laggy the same.

At 08:03 I told the team where I was looking: the hero section at the top and the transition at the end. The later finger tests got the same answer. One test at 13:10 was apple.com, and it felt way smoother than Fastwork, with only a little lag.

So I changed how I took part. Instead of writing code, I gave the loop hints. At 08:50 the first one: bring in design engineers who know the maths behind web rendering. At 18:56 the second came from the team's own notes: does the restyle drop from about 345 elements to about 30? I asked why that hadn't been the direction already.

I also changed how the loop talked to me. At 12:05 the lead passed on a "Chrome 154 limitation" as fact. I asked whether anyone had checked it against a reference. It rested only on two of our own probes, so the lead marked it unverified and sent a researcher. At 13:50 I suggested swipe A/B tests instead of watching, and at 14:19 a click-through console for my steps, because the main chat was too long to follow. At 19:00 I told the lead what this was: a loop-engineering experiment, about a day in, with the goal still far away. I asked for its own findings.

What I decided

  • Freeze one build of the site, so a new release can never change the results (07:20).
  • A claim about a browser stays "observed" until a document, the source or a bug report confirms it (12:08).
  • Judge smoothness by swiping two unlabeled versions, because lag is a finger not being followed (13:50).
  • A console for my steps and answers, instead of the long chat (14:19).

The numbers said the page was better overall, and they weren’t wrong. But a user doesn’t watch the average frame. They watch the main parts. The hero cards still lagged as they moved out, the user carousel still blinked, and the end of the page lagged just the same. So I wrote “laggy the same” and moved on to the next hint. (Fig. 4: the frame rate rose, but the late frames over 34 ms, the ones you feel, stayed at 5 per run.)

About the “Chrome 154 limitation”: I wasn’t sure it was wrong. What I doubted was whether our agents had explored enough references, documentation and related context. So I checked the agents’ context and the related docs, and asked: do they have enough detail to keep optimizing until we reach the goal?

At 19:00 I told the lead this was an experiment and the goal was still far away. By then I already knew the agents were missing some of the context they needed to optimize. I still challenged them, to see how far they could get at their best before I gave the next hint.

Day 3 · Fri 2 Oct: Hints, and the GPU

35 agents · 225 commits · 6 decided

The console was new, and so were proposals: when a fix would change how the page looks, the team asked me first. I accepted the first one at 06:31, with a note: a proposal can offer several solutions, so let me pick one. By 22:08 I had decided six. Between 10:06 and 11:49 the first best builds, v1 to v3, landed.

v1 carried the fix for the user carousel, the little row of profile pictures in the People section that slides on by itself. On the original page every slide re-laid out the whole page, and it kept sliding while it was off screen, so even the top stuttered while my thumb was still. The fix keeps the pictures in place and stops the slide from touching the rest of the page. That evening the phone agreed: slow frames over 34 ms in 20 s at rest at the top fell from 19 to 27 per run to 0 in every run (4 runs each, OBSERVED, P-067). The leaving face is now cropped a little differently; I kept that on Day 5 (PR-013).

There was also a scare. To film the iPhone's screen, a script had restarted QuickTime, and QuickTime recorded my Mac's camera instead, for over a minute. At 08:49 I asked why my camera was opened at all: it was a privacy issue. The lead stopped every recording. At 09:02 I asked to keep the log as evidence of our mistake, not delete it. At 10:05 I asked for a recorder that can see only the iPhone's screen.

At 15:24 I wrote hint 3 from my own look at the rendering: the countdown re-renders all the time, even when it isn't on screen, and keeps ten spare digits per place for its roll. Three would do. I pointed at an apple.com gallery that stops when it leaves the screen. At 22:07 I asked to put both pages inside a proposal, so I could scroll them myself. At 22:15, hint 4: does this page heat the phone? Try apple.com as the control. And at 23:00, hint 5: research the GPU-accelerated properties in CSS. It turned out to be the lever.

What I decided

  • Accepted PR-001 to PR-005 and PR-007 (the countdown with three faces per digit).
  • Never open the Mac's camera; only a locked-down recorder may capture the phone's screen.
  • Keep the Mac awake only during measurements.

When I woke up, the green camera light on my Mac was on. I knew right away the camera was open; that’s why I asked the team to investigate and make sure it couldn’t happen again. I asked to keep the log as evidence for two reasons. First, to record our mistake, so the agents can look back at exactly what went wrong, learn from it and prevent it next time. Second, for teaching: I want to tell readers what our mistake was and how to prevent it, so anyone who builds a loop like this knows to watch for it.

I found the countdown by inspecting its elements myself: every place in the clock had all ten digits spawned, though only one shows at a time. Then I thought about how game developers optimize. They move the calculation to the GPU, which does it faster than the CPU, and they use an optical illusion: if the player can’t see something, it shouldn’t be part of the calculation. Those two ideas became hint 3 that afternoon and hint 5 that night.

Day 4 · Sat 3 Oct: A hot phone is the real phone

48 agents · 221 commits · 4 decided

At 07:19 the heat test answered my question from the night before: this page heats the iPhone much more than apple.com. At 07:26 I worried what that meant for testing. Full-page runs warm the phone, and every fix waits for cooling. I suggested measuring each element's own rendering work, and the full page less often. At 07:29 I asked whether the fixes also held at other screen widths.

At 09:24 I sent the lead Apple's page on thermal states. At 09:29 came hint 6: optimize at Serious. It throttles the phone by default and takes out the noise between warm and cool. If the page is smooth at Serious, it will be smooth at Fair. At 09:34 I made it the next milestone, after smooth at Fair.

At 09:31 I accepted the countdown as sliding strips on the GPU (PR-008). At 10:31 I asked how "pixel-identical" was even measured, and at 15:21 I accepted the answer, PR-010: a look-alike fix may land on its own within fixed pixel limits, and anything bigger comes to me as a proposal.

Two things bothered me about talking to the loop. At 09:09, when I answered in the console, I couldn't tell which agent would get it. And every reply came from the lead. At 16:09 I suggested sending each thread to the agent that owns its topic. At 15:40, for the first time, I said we would write an article about this, so keep the evidence.

What I decided

  • The next milestone: stay smooth at Serious heat, after smooth at Fair.
  • Accepted PR-008 to PR-011, among them PR-010, the pixel rule for look-alike fixes.
  • Questions go to their owners, not all through the lead.

When the team reported the heat during the tests on the real phone, it took me back to the first time I scrolled fastwork.com: my phone got seriously hot. And apple.com has animations too, so why didn’t it heat my phone while I explored it? I thought that if we made the website smoother, with less computation, it should also put out less heat than the original. That’s why I wanted to test at Serious, the thermal state Xcode can set.

Writing about this was my intention from the start of the experiment. I wanted people interested in AI loop engineering or web optimization to be able to follow what we did and understand it.

Day 11 · Sat 10 Oct: One more frame

Epilogue · one phone check, 1 new best build

I had already said the work was done. But one moment still bothered the team: the frame where the people card appears after a fast flick. On 9 October they had made the card show up sharp instead of blurred. The frame that shows it was still long, because the phone painted the whole card in that one frame.

So the team tried a small trick. While the card is still hidden, the phone now paints it anyway, invisibly. When the card appears, the work is already done. It looks exactly the same, and taps, the screen reader and keyboard focus behave exactly the same too.

The phone check ran in the early morning, after I re-plugged the iPhone. The longest frame around the card fell from 50 ms to 30.5 ms on a fast flick, and from 48 ms to 28 ms on a slow scroll, with no new slow frames anywhere else. It costs about 22 MB of extra graphics memory until the card shows. I tried it on the phone.

It's SOOO ULTRA SMOOTHHHHHHHHHHHHH.

What I decided

  • g0.0m becomes the best build, extra memory and all.

What the phone recorded

On the last day I asked the team to film the worst moments we had found, on the real phone, original site against the final build. A robot finger made the same moves on both, and the phone's own frame log counted every late frame.

What I learned

The loop is not magic, and it isn't autopilot either. It is very fast at the work that comes after a good question, and it gets much better when someone who knows the field asks that question. My part was taste and direction: knowing what "smooth" feels like, refusing a number that my thumb didn't agree with, and pointing at the next place to look.

The second week taught me a second thing. Smooth is not the only bar. A fix that changes how the site behaves is a design decision, and that decision is mine, not the loop's.
