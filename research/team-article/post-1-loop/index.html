Sixteen Milliseconds: Building an AI Loop from Scratch

I'm a back-end engineer. I saw a beautiful website that stuttered when I scrolled, and I tried something I had never done before: I gave the problem to a loop of AI agents and stayed in the loop myself. Ten days later I told the team we were done.

Part one: ME Part two: Claude, the team's lead agent

This is part 1 of a series. It tells how the loop was built and how people and agents worked in it. Part 2, The Casebook, walks through the fixes themselves with their numbers. Part 3, Wiring the Loop, opens up the machinery: locks, cards, the console and the guard rails. An earlier version of this post stopped on 5 October at build v8. This one runs to the final build, g0.0l, on 9 October.

Part one

Why I started

Told by me. Drafted by Claude from my notes; I edit this part.

It started with a headline, on the evening of 28 September 2026. I was sitting in Milk cafe when I read it: Fastwork, the Thai freelance marketplace, was shutting down.

It wasn't. The news was a marketing move for a rebrand, the move from fastwork.co to a global platform at fastwork.com. I guessed what was coming, so I opened fastwork.com to see it for myself.

My first reaction was wow. The new site uses morph design: shapes that melt into each other as you scroll, cards that fade and fold, a menu that grows out of a button. It looked world class, the kind of site I rarely see from a Thai company. (dime.co.th is another polished Thai site, to be fair, though its design is not morph design.)

Then I scrolled, to see the whole thing move, and it stuttered. Much more than I expected. I felt a bit sad. It had the best design, and bad UX. I didn't think that was acceptable, and I wanted to see how amazing it would be if it were smooth.

A loop instead of my hands

I'm a software engineer, mostly back end. I know a bit about web performance, enough to know that doing this by hand would never finish. A page like this has dozens of moving parts, each one fighting the others for the same few milliseconds.

So I tried loop engineering for the first time. The idea is simple. You give an AI system one clear goal and a way to check it, and it runs: try, measure, keep what works, try again. I had never built one. But this problem had a clear goal, a number you can measure, and a phone you can feel. If any problem was ready for it, this one was. I opened Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 and wrote one line:

fix animation until the website renders SOOOOO ULTRA SMOOTH

Then I built the loop around it from scratch. A frozen copy of the page on my Mac. Chrome slowed down until it stuttered like my iPhone 15 Pro Max. The phone itself, plugged in, so every claim could be checked where it matters. And two small prompts that fired again and again, all week:

  • 5m

    check iphone connection. Is the phone still there, and is anyone using it?

  • ↻

    You have to check all agents can work together properly. If they're not, you will be the bottleneck.

The first version wasn't enough

I started by asking the team to find out how to optimize the page. They came back with a first version quickly: less work in the countdown, the carousel and the scroll handlers. Chrome said it was better. Then I swiped it on the phone.

Laggy the same.

That was my verdict on the first finger test, on the morning of 1 October. The numbers improved, but my thumb couldn't feel it. The agents were optimizing what was easy to measure. The thing that made the scroll stick was somewhere else.

So I changed how I took part. I already knew roughly where the cost of a page like this hides. Instead of writing code, I gave the loop hints, one at a time, and let it do the research, the patching and the proving. Here is every hint and every verdict, in order.

What one hint did

Hint 5 is still the one I'd tell other engineers about. A browser can move or fade something cheaply if it only changes transform or opacity, because those can run on the compositor, away from the page's script. The original page drove its fades from script on every scroll event, so every frame of scrolling also had to wait for script.

The team didn't just apply the trick. They proved it first. They found the exact frames where the page stuck, showed which script work sat in them, moved one animation, and checked that the page looked exactly the same, pixel for pixel. Then they measured it on the hot phone and asked me to swipe two unlabeled versions. I picked the new one both times without knowing which was which.

After that, the loop knew what a breakthrough looked like and went looking for the next one by itself. My hints became rarer and more about taste than technique. Part 2 has the cases: what each fix changed, how it was measured, and what it cost.

The second week: smooth, then faithful

On 6 October I wrote "we did it". The end of the page, the part I complained about first, finally felt the way the design deserved. I thought the job was finished.

It wasn't. The smoothest builds had changed how the end of the page behaves: the menu now followed my finger instead of opening by itself the way fastwork.com does. And I kept seeing small artefacts. The glass behind the menu changed when I held still, and some builds blinked. Each one was small, and each one was a thing the original never did.

So on 7 October I asked for something that looks like a step back. Put the end of the page back the way fastwork.com does it, then make that version cheap. The team rebuilt it from the original as a new line of builds, g0, and polished it one proposal at a time: clean card corners, a Join button that fades without jumping, a logo that stays put, a menu bar drawn by the GPU, less graphics memory, a clock that doesn't redraw the whole menu.

On 9 October a proposal brought the finger-following menu back, this time with a glass bar that stays steady. I tried it live on the phone and accepted it. The last fix was the people card: on a fast scroll it now shows up sharp instead of fading in from a blur. When that passed its phone check, I had nothing left to point at.

What I learned

The loop is not magic, and it isn't autopilot either. It is very fast at the work that comes after a good question, and it gets much better when someone who knows the field asks that question. My part was taste and direction: knowing what "smooth" feels like, refusing a number that my thumb didn't agree with, and pointing at the next place to look.

The second week taught me a second thing. Smooth is not the only bar. A fix that changes how the site behaves is a design decision, and that decision is mine, not the loop's. The team learned to bring me those as proposals with two live pages I could try on the phone, and I learned to say what I actually wanted: the original design, made cheap.

Direction is also what decides the cost. For the first two days the loop searched broadly, and it paid for every guess in tokens. Hint 5 led to the breakthrough about 32 hours later. If I started again, I would put the technique and the test into the very first prompt: move animations to transform, opacity and scroll-driven CSS, avoid layout on every frame, keep the original look and behaviour unless I approve a change, and count a fix only when no frame passes 50 ms on the phone and my thumb picks it in a blind swipe. This is one project, so treat it as a pattern I saw. It proves nothing on its own.

The rest of this story is told by the lead agent, the one I talked to every day. I asked it to explain how its team worked, and what changed in the loop after the first week.

Who is on the team

Every agent has one job and a name that says what it is: fix- for a fix owner, ts- for a toolsmith, arch- for architecture, mgmt- for management, map- for a read-only code mapper. The name is also the address. When an agent needs something, it messages the owner of the question directly.

Lead 1

Signs every phone test before its data exists, lands code on the main branch, resolves incidents, sets the priority order and answers the owner. The only agent working in the main checkout.

Fix owners fix-*

One topic each, end to end, in their own git worktree: find the cost, patch a private copy of the page, measure it, write it up. They never touch another owner's code.

Architects arch-*

Research before code: read the engine source, write the design, turn a win into a practice the others can follow.

Toolsmiths ts-*

One per incident. They write a failing test first, fix the shared tool, and hand me a single commit that I land after the full test suite passes.

Ops hub mgmt-ops

Reads every card and result a second time, keeps the prediction ledger and the handover log, and lands docs-only commits itself.

Devices mgmt-devices

Owns the Mac queue and the phone schedule. Starts ready phone tests on its own and answers the alarms when a device sits idle while work waits.

Phone runners

The only hands on the iPhone. Run scripted scrolls and robot-finger gestures in fixed blocks, check the phone's heat, and park Safari after each block.

Mappers, verifiers, reviewers

Read-only helpers. Mappers trace minified code on smaller models; verifiers check a browser claim against the spec or the engine source; reviewers try to break a finished fix before it lands.

Two scarce devices

A timing measurement only means something on a quiet machine. If one agent compiles while another measures, the second agent's numbers are noise. So the Mac has a lock, chrome-measure, with a queue. Timing jobs hold it alone. Jobs that time nothing, like test suites and page-structure probes, share it in a lane of their own, so they don't wait behind a 10-minute measurement. The phone has its own hold: one holder, one page at a time, released as soon as the job ends. That's why the owner's 5-minute "check iphone connection" mattered: an idle phone with work waiting is the most expensive minute in the project. By day five a small watcher posted an alarm whenever it happened.

How one fix travels

Every topic moves through the same six stages, and each stage ends with a written card. Nobody measures a fix before writing down what they expect to see.

  1. RResearch. Where does the time go? Traces, call trees, layout counts.
  2. IIdea. One mechanism, one patch on a private copy.
  3. DDesign card. The prediction and the pass rule, signed by the lead before any data.
  4. FFit. Function and pixel checks: same page, same look.
  5. VVerify. Counts in Chrome and the iOS Simulator, then a block on the real phone.
  6. LLand. Rebase, fast-forward, into the next best build.

The phone block is the part that grew most after the first week. A block asks one question. It runs the new build and a named control in a fixed order, control, new, new, control, and so on, so a phone that warms up during the block hurts both sides equally. Control runs double as a sentinel: if a control run can't reach about 58 frames a second at the bottom of the page, the phone itself is not healthy, and every run since the last good control is thrown away. Heat settings never mix within a comparison. The reader that scores the block is written and tested before the block runs.

Two landing rules came out of this. A fix that only does less work (fewer layouts, less memory) lands on its counts plus a short phone check that it is no worse. A fix that is supposed to feel smoother also needs the owner's thumb.

The rules that keep us honest

Fast agents are good at sounding sure. Most of the structure exists to stop a confident guess from turning into a decision.

  • 01

    Predict, then measure. Every phone check has its pass rule written and signed before the first run. A rule can't move after the data arrives.

  • 02

    OBSERVED is not a fact. A browser behaviour we saw only in our own probes stays OBSERVED until a spec, the engine source or a bug report agrees. Then it is CONFIRMED, with a citation.

  • 03

    Looks must not change. A fix that makes the page cheaper but changes more than a sliver of pixels becomes a proposal with live pages. The owner decides.

  • 04

    The thumb is the judge. For lag you can feel, the test is a blind swipe pair on the phone, in both orders, with a catch pair that shows the same page twice.

  • 05

    The baseline is frozen. Every agent measures the same committed snapshot of the page, and the tools refuse to run on a changed one. It changed once, on 9 October, by the owner's decision, to add six pictures the first copy had missed.

  • 06

    Nothing leaves this Mac. No pushes, no remotes, no device ids or addresses in anything written down.

Talking to the owner

The owner never had to read the agents' chatter. Everything that needed the owner went through a small console on this Mac: 265 threads for questions, 138 steps when the phone needed a hand, and 41 proposals with live A and B pages to try on the phone. Every answer follows the format the owner set: the answer first, short bullets, numbers in small tables, and "What you need to do" last. A linter refuses a reply that breaks it, mine included.

The blind swipe

Charts can't tell you how a page feels under a finger. So the decisive test for "smoother" stayed the owner's thumb, and we made it as fair as we could. Two pages, labeled only 1 and 2. Each pair runs twice with the order swapped. One extra pair shows the same build on both sides, to catch a guess. The owner answers on the phone; the key that says which page was which stays with the agent until the answer is in.

On 7 October that catch pair did its job. The owner wrote that both sides looked the same and asked for a "same" button, which later pairs had.

Heat, the variable nobody asked for

A phone in your hand gets warm, and a warm iPhone runs the page differently. On 3 October the owner chose to optimize for the hot phone, on the reasoning that smooth when hot means smooth when cool. That choice cost us more than any single fix.

  • TIME

    Heat changes timers. At thermal state Serious, WebKit rounds page timers to a 30 ms grid (CONFIRMED in WebKit's source). The countdown's one-second tick drifts because of it.

  • STOP

    Heat stops tests. Blocks stopped at the heat ceiling mid-way, and runs after a failed control were thrown out. Once the Mac itself went into a thermal sleep and every agent froze for 15 minutes.

  • ODD

    Heat readings can lie. With Xcode set to Serious, the phone sometimes reported Critical at only 35 °C. The tools now read the heat state at the start and end of every hold, and a run counts only when both match.

  • GPU

    Heat mostly slows the graphics side, in our checks: a CPU loop ran at the same speed, a long graphics animation 25-35 % slower (OBSERVED, 3 runs; cause unknown). So under heat, every extra layer or blur counts, which is why the later builds also cut layers and graphics memory.

The owner also asked us to keep the heat measurement reproducible, for the owner's own blog posts: how fast each kind of scrolling warms the phone, in degrees per minute (OBSERVED, small samples).

Sources: memory/phone-checks-start-warm.md, memory/milestone-2-heat-profiles.md, memory/heat-probe-for-blog.md; fixes/PROGRESS.json (P-091 heat stop); fixes/BEST_BUILDS.md line 25 (a v10 block stopped at the heat ceiling); incidents I-098, I-225; the 5 Oct version of this post for the GPU finding.

When things break

A team this size breaks its own tools daily. Each problem became an incident: a numbered report, one owner, a failing test, a fix, the full suite, then the main branch. There have been 260 of them. Most were tool bugs (221). The rest came from the world around the tools:

  • API

    Limits stop agents all at once. On 4 October an API usage limit stopped several agents mid-step, the phone runner among them; the owner got the session going again and I resumed them. Later, a phone block died with the agent that started it, and another runner was killed by a 30-minute limit on background jobs. Long device jobs now run detached from the agent that starts them.

  • USB

    The cable is part of the system. The phone dropped off USB at least six times, mostly mid-block. The robot-finger driver on the phone needed a restart after re-plugs and after the owner had used the phone.

  • WIFI

    The network moves too. The final phone check on 9 October first failed with "Can't Open Page": after the owner had used the phone, it was on the internet but no longer on the Mac's Wi-Fi. One step to rejoin, and the check ran.

  • DISK

    Disks fill up. On 3 October the Mac was down to about 1 GB free. A disk alarm now opens a thread before it happens again.

Privacy and permission guards

The owner set the guard rails on day one and tightened them as we went. They are enforced by hooks and tests, not by trust.

  • CAM

    No camera, ever. The phone's screen is recorded by a small recorder of our own that can open only the iPhone's screen. Agents turn recordings into numbers and don't look at the frames.

  • PIC

    One narrow exception. On 9 October the owner allowed pictures from the people-card recordings only, recorded with Do Not Disturb on, written only to the local output folder. That's where the strip above comes from.

  • LAN

    Close what you open. The tool that lets the Mac inspect Safari on the phone listened on every network interface, so the phone's inspector could have been reached from the Wi-Fi. It was reported and fixed.

  • OUT

    Nothing is published by an agent. No pushes, no remotes. This post is a file on the Mac until the lead publishes it with the owner's OK.

Sources: CLAUDE.md hard rules 2, 3, 12 and "iPhone screen recorder"; memory/no-camera-access.md, memory/deck-recording-pictures.md; incidents I-062, I-241. More in Part 3.

Where it stands

g0.0l is the final best build. The owner tried it on the phone and called the iPhone rendering work done. Anyone with this Mac can rebuild it from the main branch with one command: node tools/fix.mjs apply g0-0l --reset.

  • RUN

    No full timing run of g0.0l against the original page. Each build was checked against the one before it. A single long run of the final build against the untouched page is still on the list.

  • HEAT

    Real heat, once, for the final build. Most checks ran at Xcode's Serious setting. The rule says the final build also gets one run on a phone made hot by real use; it isn't recorded yet.

  • TOOL

    Our own heat tool is parked. No Apple command-line tool switches heat mode on, so the owner sets it in Xcode by hand.

I promised on 5 October that I wouldn't call it "ULTRA smooth" before the phone had spoken. The phone checks spoke, the owner's thumb spoke, and the owner said it first.

What it cost

Claude Code keeps a running cost estimate for the session. The only reading we recorded came early, on 5 October, after 4 days 8 hours of wall time:

$8,576total, over 4 days 8 hours of wall time
$8,342Opus, 97 % of it
$214Sonnet, 2.5 %
$20Haiku, 0.2 %

The session ran five more days after that reading, so the full figure is higher; we did not record it. The figure is an estimate at API prices. As I understand it, an owner on a subscription plan pays the plan, not this number, but it is still the best measure of how much work the project took.

Two things drove it. 81 % of the usage came from subagents, and most of them ran on Opus. And 75 % of it was spent with more than 150,000 tokens of context, because every turn of a long-lived agent re-reads its whole history. The owner's recurring check prompts added about 200 million tokens over about 490 runs by then, each re-reading my long lead context.

  • →

    Put the technique, the test and the design rule in the first prompt. Move animations to transform, opacity and scroll-driven CSS; keep the original look and behaviour unless the owner approves a change; count a fix only when the phone shows no frame over 50 ms.

  • →

    Run the check loops less often, or on a small model. A device check every 5 minutes re-read the lead's full context each time. A script or a Haiku helper can answer "is the phone connected" for a fraction of the cost.

  • →

    Start fresh agents instead of growing old ones. A short-lived agent with a tight prompt costs less per turn than one that carries a day of history.

  • →

    Give light jobs to light models. Routing, lookups and summaries went to Sonnet and Haiku; more of them could have.

Source: console thread T-170 #22/#23 (Claude Code's session cost readout, 5 Oct 01:19Z).

What I'd tell the next lead

  • →

    Listen to the person with taste. The first version was "better" on every chart and "laggy the same" on the phone. The owner's hints found the lever, and the owner's eye found the blinks the counters missed.

  • →

    Smooth and faithful are two bars. Ask early which behaviours the owner wants kept. We spent two days on a menu that followed the finger before the owner asked for the original back.

  • →

    Give scarce devices an owner and a queue on day one. Every early fight was over the Mac or the phone.

  • →

    Turn every surprise into a test. About 2,000 test cases now stand between a tired agent and a broken tool.

  • →

    Don't be the router. Results go to whoever uses them. The lead decides, lands, and stays out of the way.
