122: Before day one
123: It started with a headline, on the evening of 28 September 2026. I was sitting in Milk cafe when I read it: Fastwork, the Thai freelance marketplace, was shutting down.
124: It wasn't. The news was a marketing move for a rebrand, the move from fastwork.co to a global platform at fastwork.com. I guessed what was coming, so I opened fastwork.com to see it for myself.
125: The new site uses morph design: shapes that melt into each other as you scroll, cards that fade and fold, a menu that grows out of a button. My first wow came from the headline at the top, the “Show the world what you can do” animation.
126: That animation was quite smooth. Then I did my first slow scroll to see the other animations. The hero cards moved out so laggy. So did everything after them: “People still need people”, the user carousel, all the way to the end of the page. On my iPhone 15 Pro Max it was so laggy.
127: That is what made me want to fix it. I wanted to see how amazing this website would be with smooth animation on my iPhone.
128: How I measure
130: My iPhone shows a new picture every 16.7 ms. For each picture, the page has to finish its own work in time.
131: A slow frame took the page over 34 ms, so at least one picture is lost. A very slow frame took over 50 ms. My goal is no very slow frames. Slow frames are the count I tracked most.
132: The counts come from the phone’s own frame log in Safari, stamped to the millisecond. Each count names its window: the seconds of a clip, or a whole run.
133: Who moves the page: my own finger; the robot replaying my swipe; the robot’s slow drags (about 400 to 500 px, then a rest); one fast robot flick; robot pushes at the end of the page; or nobody, with the page at rest.
134: Heat: the phone tests ran at Serious, the iPhone’s second-hottest thermal state, set by a tool unless I write “real heat”. A run that drifted to Critical, the hottest state, is shown as an example only.

181: Day 1 · Wed 30 Sep: One complaint, one goal
182: 72 agents · 125 commits · no proposals yet
183: At 17:05 UTC, just after midnight my time, I asked Claude how to connect Safari on my iPhone to the Mac. By 17:53 I had scrolled the page with the inspector attached, and I wrote my first observation in plain words: laggy at the top and at the end. Those two places stayed the targets for the whole ten days.
184: Three minutes later I mentioned something I had already tried on my own: Chrome on the desktop, with the CPU slowed down 4x, stuttered too. Was it the same code? I wanted the simplest way to reproduce the phone’s lag on my MacBook Pro M1, and to explain it, so I wouldn’t have to ask for a test on the real iPhone every time. That question became the plan. Copy the page to my Mac, then slow Chrome until it lags like the phone. The team calibrated it to 6x.
185: I'm a software engineer, mostly back end. I knew a few web animation optimization tricks, but I have a full-time job, so I had no time to fix a page like this by hand. So I asked a different question: if I build an AI loop, give it a goal and a few clues, and let the agents investigate, measure, probe and fix by themselves, can AI get there on its own? That was the experiment, my first try at loop engineering, built from scratch. The AI team had to investigate and optimize the site’s compiled source, the files fastwork.com actually ships, without rewriting the page from scratch. I didn’t care about code quality, only the rendering result: could it get SOOO ULTRA SMOOTHHHH?
186: It ran on a fixed budget: Claude Max, the $200 plan, on 3 to 5 accounts. In this experiment I used three, carrying the work over between them as each one’s weekly usage ran out.
187: I added the pieces through the evening. At 19:33, one agent per problem, each in its own copy of the code. At 20:00, tests for every tool. At 20:17, a hard rule: rebase only. At 21:14 I made the lead the orchestrator until the job was done, and at 21:21 I set the goal in one line:
188: fix animation until the website renders SOOOOO ULTRA SMOOTH
189: The capitals are a joke. “ULTRA SMOOTHHHHHH” is a viral food and marketing trend in Thailand: an Italian gelato with an ultra-fine, slow-melting texture, made popular by the brand Parameter.
190: It was not smooth. At 21:49 I saw uncommitted work on main; a minute later I asked why nobody was using Chrome, and later why agents had to queue to commit. The Mac reached a load of 58. At 23:05 I told the lead it was being interrupted a lot and had to delegate. At 23:26 I asked why it needed me to spot these problems. I had seen lead agents in other AI setups fail to rebase and land work on the main branch, so I wanted that part solid before anything else.
