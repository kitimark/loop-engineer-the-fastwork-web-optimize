125: Sixteen Milliseconds
126: Ten days, one phone, one goal: how I built an AI loop from scratch and steered it until a stuttering page felt smooth under my thumb.
127: Part one · Told by me, drafted by Claude from my notes and the log
128: Every fix, measurement and finger test aimed at one setup: Safari on my iPhone 15 Pro Max. Times are UTC. Phone numbers marked OBSERVED come from our own checks, in our setup.
130: Before day one
131: It started with a headline, on the evening of 28 September 2026. I was sitting in Milk cafe when I read it: Fastwork, the Thai freelance marketplace, was shutting down.
132: It wasn't. The news was a marketing move for a rebrand, the move from fastwork.co to a global platform at fastwork.com. I guessed what was coming, so I opened fastwork.com to see it for myself.
133: The new site uses morph design: shapes that melt into each other as you scroll, cards that fade and fold, a menu that grows out of a button. My first wow came from the headline at the top, the “Show the world what you can do” animation.

144: [h3{id=d1}] Day 1 · Wed 30 Sep: One complaint, one goal
145: 72 agents · 125 commits
146: At 17:05 UTC, just after midnight my time, I asked Claude how to connect Safari on my iPhone to the Mac. By 17:53 I had scrolled the page with the inspector attached, and I wrote my first observation in plain words: laggy at the top and at the end. Those two places stayed the targets for the whole ten days.
147: Three minutes later I mentioned something I had already tried on my own: Chrome on the desktop, with the CPU slowed down 4x, stuttered too. Was it the same code? That question became the plan. Copy the page to my Mac, then slow Chrome until it lags like the phone. The team calibrated it to 6x.
148: I'm a software engineer, mostly back end. I knew a few web animation optimization tricks, but I have a full-time job, so I had no time to fix a page like this by hand. So I asked a different question: if I build an AI loop, give it a goal and a few clues, and let the agents investigate, measure, probe and fix by themselves, can AI get there on its own? That was the experiment, my first try at loop engineering, built from scratch. The AI team had to investigate and optimize the site’s compiled source, the files fastwork.com actually ships, without rewriting the page from scratch. I didn’t care about code quality, only the rendering result: could it get SOOO ULTRA SMOOTHHHH? It ran on a fixed budget: Claude Max, the $200 plan, on 3 to 5 accounts. In this experiment I used three, carrying the work over between them as each one’s weekly usage ran out. I added the pieces through the evening. At 19:33, one agent per problem, each in its own copy of the code. At 20:00, tests for every tool. At 20:17, a hard rule: rebase only. At 21:14 I made the lead the orchestrator until the job was done, and at 21:21 I set the goal in one line:
149: fix animation until the website renders SOOOOO ULTRA SMOOTH
150: It was not smooth. At 21:49 I saw uncommitted work on main; a minute later I asked why nobody was using Chrome, and later why agents had to queue to commit. The Mac reached a load of 58. At 23:05 I told the lead it was being interrupted a lot and had to delegate. At 23:26 I asked why it needed me to spot these problems.

205: What I decided
207: Study a local copy only; never touch the live site (18:06).
208: One agent per topic, each in its own worktree; rebase only, no merge commits (19:33, 20:17).
209: The lead orchestrates until done, and must delegate instead of doing everything itself (21:14, 23:05).
211: I already knew a few ways to measure rendering, and slowing Chrome’s CPU 4x was one of them. What I wanted was the simplest way to reproduce the phone’s lag on my MacBook Pro M1, and to explain it, so I wouldn’t have to ask for a test on the real iPhone every time.
212: And the capitals in my goal are a joke. “ULTRA SMOOTHHHHHH” is a viral food and marketing trend in Thailand: an Italian gelato with an ultra-fine, slow-melting texture, made popular by the brand Parameter.
213: At 23:26 I pointed out the dirty main branch and the commit queue myself. I just wanted to prevent an issue I had run into in other AI environments: the lead agent couldn’t rebase and land work on the main branch.

300: What I decided
302: Accepted PR-001 to PR-005 and PR-007 (the countdown with three faces per digit).
303: Never open the Mac's camera; only a locked-down recorder may capture the phone's screen.
304: Keep the Mac awake only during measurements.
306: When I woke up, the green camera light on my Mac was on. I knew right away the camera was open; that’s why I asked the team to investigate and make sure it couldn’t happen again. I asked to keep the log as evidence for two reasons. First, to record our mistake, so the agents can look back at exactly what went wrong, learn from it and prevent it next time. Second, for teaching: I want to tell readers what our mistake was and how to prevent it, so anyone who builds a loop like this knows to watch for it.
307: I found the countdown by inspecting its elements myself: every place in the clock had all ten digits spawned, though only one shows at a time. Then I thought about how game developers optimize. They move the calculation to the GPU, which does it faster than the CPU, and they use an optical illusion: if the player can’t see something, it shouldn’t be part of the calculation. Those two ideas became hint 3 that afternoon and hint 5 that night.

557: What I learned
558: The loop is not magic, and it isn't autopilot either. It is very fast at the work that comes after a good question, and it gets much better when someone who knows the field asks that question. My part was taste and direction: knowing what "smooth" feels like, refusing a number that my thumb didn't agree with, and pointing at the next place to look.
559: The second week taught me a second thing. Smooth is not the only bar. A fix that changes how the site behaves is a design decision, and that decision is mine, not the loop's.
