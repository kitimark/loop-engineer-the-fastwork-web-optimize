Why a beautiful page stutters

2026-10-12. A personal, educational study of the public homepage https://fastwork.com/ on a frozen local copy (one build, pinned by baseline.json). Not affiliated with or endorsed by Fastwork. This is not a criticism of the people who built the page: it is ambitious design work, and most of what follows is invisible on the machines a web team usually tests on.

Conventions. "Phone" is an iPhone 15 Pro Max with Safari on iOS 26.6.1. "Chrome 6x" is Chrome at 430x932, DPR 3, touch, with the CPU slowed 6x. "Simulator" is the iOS Simulator; we used it for counts only. A "slow" frame takes over 34 ms, which loses at least one 60 Hz picture. A "very slow" frame takes over 50 ms. JS and CSS lines are 1-based lines of the prettified baseline (site-pretty/_app/immutable/nodes/2.DyWL8TcE.js, .../assets/2.gBuqKVzi.css). Every timing and count in this document is OBSERVED: our own runs, in our setup, usually 2 to 5 runs per arm. Platform claims carry the status from the verifier's table (section 6). "Inference" marks a guess, mostly about why the original team wrote what it wrote; the repo has no record of their process.

How to read the math

Each section and most lessons carry a box marked The math. It holds one plain sentence, the formula, a worked example with our own numbers, a chart drawn to scale from those numbers, and the repo path the numbers come from.

  • A frame is one picture. At 60 Hz the phone has 1000 / 60 = 16.67 ms per picture; a frame over 34 ms loses at least one picture, over 50 ms at least two.
  • The chip says how far to trust a line: CONFIRMED a spec, engine source or bug agrees; OBSERVED our own runs only, in our setup; DERIVED arithmetic or a model built on our numbers; UNRESOLVED no reference settles it.
  • In the charts, orange is the original page, green an intermediate build and blue the fix or final build, unless a chart's own legend says otherwise. Every bar carries its value, so the colour is never the only cue. In frame splits the stages have their own colours: Style, Layout, Composite, with Paint hatched inside Composite.

1. Summary

  • No single thing was slow. Dozens of effects that are each cheap alone shared the same frames on a 60 Hz phone, and removing one usually moved the cost to the next (lessons 1, 30).
  • Mistake families:
    • (a) script reading and writing layout every scroll frame;
    • (b) animating properties the engine cannot composite (width, grid columns, stroke-dashoffset, background-position, SMIL, steps());
    • (c) WebKit-only layout traps (presence flips, sticky elements under the moving iOS toolbar, size containers);
    • (d) layer mistakes (resized composited layers, a layer per digit, layers built in an interaction's first frame, full-screen blurs);
    • (e) loops nobody can see;
    • (f) scroll-jacking.
  • Why it happens: the costs live in an engine and device the team does not develop on. Chrome at 1x (mobile emulation) showed 0.0 slow frames per scripted scroll where the phone showed 15.7 (lesson 23). The cost rules differ per engine and are not in CSS tutorials, and the usual metrics (fps averages, LoAF) miss the frames a thumb feels.
  • The result: on the phone, replaying the user's own swipe through the top of the page, slow frames fell from 20.1 % to 1.7 % and very slow frames from 10.1 % to 0.22 %. At the end of the page, the longest frame fell from 584 ms to 42 ms. Above the end of the page the final build keeps the original look. It differs in two places the user chose: at the end the menu follows the finger instead of opening by itself, and after a fast flick the People card shows at once instead of fading in (section 2, lessons 14, 21).
  • We made the same kinds of mistakes while fixing it: we trusted Chrome counts, scored scripted scrolls, blamed the most visible animation, and let a single probe become "fact". The user's thumb caught what the numbers missed (lessons 23-28).
  • Rule 1: test on the slowest real target (here a 60 Hz iPhone with Safari) from day one, with a real finger gesture, warm, and count frames over 34 and 50 ms per scroll zone, not fps averages.
  • Rule 2: animate only transform and opacity (and filter with care) of layers that already exist, driven by CSS or scroll timelines, not by script writing styles every frame.
  • Rule 3: in a frame that moves, script reads no layout and writes nothing that inherits. Read once, write once, and prefer observers to rect reads.
  • Rule 4: every animation has an owner for "can anyone see this?" and goes to zero work at rest. Test the page parked at every scroll position.
  • Rule 5: price every layer and blur (MB, first-frame cost, Composite time), create layers before the motion starts, and never resize a composited or blurred layer in motion.

The mathThe 60 Hz picture grid: what "over 34" and "over 50" meanCONFIRMED MODEL

The phone's screen can show a new picture only every 16.67 ms. A frame that takes longer leaves the old picture up for one or more ticks. That is a missed picture, and it is what a thumb feels as a stutter.

P=1000 ms60=16.67 ms missed=max(0,round(dt/P)−1)dropped=∑missed∑missed+frames

dt > 34 sits 0.7 ms above 2P = 33.3: at least one picture missed. dt > 50 sits on 3P = 50.0: about two missed.

Worked example, our numbers

  • Three scripted phone runs miss 69, 49, 51 refreshes in 351, 358, 358 frames: dropped 16.4 %, 12.0 %, 12.5 %, mean 13.6 %.
  • The 18 frames over 34 ms of run 1: 36, 36, 37, 38, 40, 40, 41, 42, 44, 47, 50, 51, 51, 59, 64, 71, 75, 111 ms. They spread across the gaps between ticks (one lands on 50 = 3P); whether phone gaps cluster on the grid is settled by the 198,349-gap analysis in the caption, not by 18 frames.

The mathHeat rate in °C per minute, over a blank floor pageRULE OBSERVED

How fast a page warms the phone is a slope: battery temperature against time. A hotter start warms more slowly, so each slope is corrected to a 37 °C start, and a blank page's slope is subtracted to leave what the page itself adds.

scorr=s+k·(T‾−37.0),k=0.045/minexcess=scorr(page)−scorr(floor)removed=B−MB−F

s = least-squares slope of battery temperature over 60-360 s (11 reads); k = 0.045 /min, between the load fit's 0.040 (slope = 1.84 − 0.040 × start) and the cooling fit's 0.052

Worked example, our numbers

  • Corrected slopes, two sessions (real heat, 11 Oct): floor F −0.198 / −0.221; original B +0.269 / +0.270; g0.0m M +0.057 / +0.115 °C/min.
  • Excess: B 0.269 + 0.198 = 0.467 and 0.270 + 0.221 = 0.491; M 0.255 / 0.336. Removed: 0.212 / 0.467 = 45 % and 0.155 / 0.491 = 32 %.
  • In plain units, over the fitted 5-minute window: the original warms the phone 2.3-2.5 °C more than the blank page, g0.0m 1.3-1.7 °C.
  • Decision rule, set before the data: "cooler" when B − X ≥ +0.05 °C/min in both sessions; one session's noise is up to about 0.085 (√2 × 0.061). B − M = +0.212 / +0.155: cooler in both. v6 − M = 0.000 / −0.017: no detectable change.

A. Script on every frame

01Read, write, read in the shared scroll callbacks.

  • Original. One scroll engine (rE/iE/dE, JS 2663-2780) runs every effect's callback in one rAF. The callbacks mix rect reads with style writes, so each read after a write forces a synchronous style and layout pass. The nav's hit test probes the page on every frame by writing an inherited property, which restyles its whole subtree twice: T.style.pointerEvents = 'none'; document.elementFromPoint(...); T.style.pointerEvents = r (JS 12955-12963).
  • Cost. Chrome 6x: the nav callback ran 316-320 times and 526-617 ms per run; iE took ~55 ms over 3 frames (fixes/TOPICS.md). On the phone, before fixes/hit-skip, the control laid out 2.2-2.4 times per morph frame.
  • Why. Each effect was written as its own component with a sensible rect read and write. The cost is spread over 20-30 ms frames that LoAF never reports. (Inference.)
  • Fixed by. elementsFromPoint() skipping its own subtree (0/1690 parity mismatches); cached MediaQueryList and querySelector results; writing only on change; skipping reads whose answer cannot show.
  • Did not work. Removing one reader. Removing the pain section's 333 rect reads cut iE's forced layout from 46-52 to 4-7 ms, but the next reader rose from 15-20 to 25-30 ms (net -24 ms, within noise; fixes/FINDINGS.md). On WebKit, gating off-screen callbacks left forced style passes at 230-243 (fixes/scrollcb-g0). The nav's window.scrollY read paid instead: when scrollY is not 0, reading it makes WebKit run any pending layout first (CONFIRMED, LocalDOMWindow.cpp 1508-1512). That costs only when layout is dirty, which in a callback that also writes styles is every frame.
  • Rule. Per frame, all reads first, then all writes, once. Never write an inherited property to probe the DOM. A removed read pays only when every reader in that frame is gone.
The mathForced layout: order, not countCONFIRMED MODEL OBSERVED

A layout read is nearly free when nothing is pending. A read right after a style write must first run all the pending style and layout work. So the cost is set by how many times the code switches from writing to reading, and the first dirty reader pays for everyone: remove it and the next reader pays instead.

T=G·carith+R·cread+ndirty·Tflushndirty=# write → read switches

G arithmetic steps, R clean reads; reading a non-zero scrollY in WebKit also runs pending layout

Worked example, our numbers

  • Apple's page: 440 clean reads per frame with 0 ms forced. The iPhone 18 Pro page does the same reads after a write: 12-25 ms per event.
  • Ours, Chrome 6x: the nav scroll handler ran 316-320 calls and took 526-617 ms per run, 1.64-1.95 ms per call (derived, ranges unpaired), 2-4 ms per frame.
  • Moved, not removed: the pain section's reads went 333 → 0 per pass; iE's forced layout fell 46-52 → 4-7 ms, but the next reader rose 15-20 → 25-30 ms; net −24 ms, within noise.
  • Phone, robot finger: layouts per morph frame 2.23-2.38 → 1.00-1.04. Of 291 layouts forced in script over 922 Simulator frames, 158 came from elementsFromPoint and 44 from window.scrollY.

02One inherited custom property drives a whole subtree.

  • Original. The hero exit (JS 4163-4172) reads .hero-track's rect and writes --exit-opacity on .hero. CSS turns it into opacity, translate and blur on the copy, cards and scroll cue. At the end of the page, a tween writes --morph on the nav every frame (JS 13021-13036). Both are unregistered, so every write restyles the subtree.
  • Cost. Hero (~230 elements): a modelled 5-12 ms per frame, mostly style (derived, research/design-engineering/hero.md).
  • Why. One scalar driving everything is the idiomatic CSS-variable pattern, and it is fine at 120 Hz on a fast Mac. (Inference.)
  • Tried first. A CSS view-timeline with animation-range: contain 0% contain 100%, which equals the JS formula, with a JS @supports fallback (FINDINGS T12). It removed every JS write in Chrome. On the phone it won at y ~50 (max-frame median 48 → 35 ms) and lost at y ~700 (51 → 63 ms, disjoint ranges). That was a new WebKit cost, not a moved one; one hypothesis is the seven live ViewTimeline animations past the flip, cause UNRESOLVED (postmortem-hero.md card 1).
  • Shipped. v6 moved the cards' fade to the compositor, scroll-driven instead of script-written every frame (fixes/hero-exit-threaded). Slow frames in the hero band went from a median of 27 to 12 per run against v4, but the ranges overlap (27, 37, 22 against 26, 8, 12), so the verdict was "no worse". The user picked v6 in both blind swipes. On the 11 Oct re-run the medians were 31 (original), 18 (v4) and 7 (v6), with v6 against v4 threshold-sensitive (article Fig. 7).
  • Did not work.
    • Registering --morph with inherits: false left the nav's children stale on WebKit (fixes/morph-inherit).
    • In our setup, Safari 26 keyframes that reference an animated custom property resolved against its base value (FINDINGS P3).
  • Rule. No custom property as a per-frame animation bus. Animate real properties where they are used, and trace the target engine at every scroll position, including where the range ends.

  • Budget: 3 ms / c_el = 120-300 elements per frame. One 0.72 frame restyled 429 elements → 4.3-10.7 ms (derived).

  • Reality check: at the hero flip the phone showed style of about 5 ms (1.9 + 3.1), at the bottom of the predicted 5-29, not inside it. Layout, not style, was the term the model missed.

03Script drives effects that CSS switched off, or that no longer exist.

  • Original. "Shrink" effects (--future-shrink, --commission-*) ease 22 % per frame in JS, reading rects, while phone CSS forces scale 1 and radius 0 (CSS 916-938). The hero CTA's orbit animates an element that is always display: none (CSS 1821-1828).
  • Cost. Gating the shrinks on phones took Chrome 6x from 66.5 to 71.6 fps; phone frames over 20 ms fell from 60-64 to 40-43 (FINDINGS T1).
  • Why. One component serves desktop and phone, responsive behaviour lives in CSS, and the JS never learns the same media query. Effects get hidden, not deleted. (Inference.)
  • Fixed by. Gating on exactly the CSS media query (every comma part), rerun on the MQL change event, keeping side jobs other code reads.
  • Also. A state write is not free when it changes nothing. Svelte's set compares with ===, so z(T, [], true) with a fresh [] reran derived blocks on every frame (FINDINGS P9).
  • Did not work. A replace-all rename of a minified name (cy) broke six SVG circles, unseen by three screenshots (FINDINGS P1).
  • Rule. When CSS disables an effect, the JS reads the same condition. Inventory what really runs (getAnimations(), counters you have seen fire) before ranking fixes. Delete instead of hiding.

On phones CSS had switched the effect off, so all those frames did work for nothing.   • Gating the shrinks on phones took Chrome 6x from 66.5 to 71.6 fps, i.e. 15.04 → 13.97 ms per frame (−1.07 ms, derived). Phone frames over 20 ms fell from 60-64 to 40-43.

04Two drivers on one property.

  • Original. JS eases the future card's --future-shrink while its 0.9 s transform transition stays on. Every frame retargets the transition (CONFIRMED, CSS Transitions spec), so the card trails by a further 0.7-1.3 s. A class keyed on the raw target flips transform-origin mid-scale, and the card jumps up to 270.9 px on scroll-up (Chrome desktop probe, fixes/double-drive).
  • Why. An entrance transition and a later scroll-linked value are each right alone; nobody owns how they interact. (Inference.)
  • Rule. One driver per property. If script writes it every frame, it gets transition: none, and class thresholds key on the eased value. When a tween becomes finger-driven, re-check every sub-motion at the new speed.

05Layout properties in decoration.

4. Why these mistakes happen

Root causes across the lessons. E marks evidence from the repo; I marks inference.

1. The team sees a different machine than the user. Chrome at 1x showed 0.0 slow frames per scripted scroll in mobile emulation (0.3 on desktop), where the phone showed 15.7. The morph ran at 110 fps in Chrome 6x and at a p50 of 29-68 ms per frame on the phone (driver to finger) (E: lessons 11 and 23). That the original team developed mainly in desktop Chromium is I. 2. Cost is per engine and per pipeline stage, and the rules are not in tutorials. WebKit relayouts on presence flips and on dashoffset changes, discards backing stores on resize, and keeps steps() on the main thread. Chrome refuses to composite blur animations. All of these are CONFIRMED by engine source, and none is visible in the stylesheet (E: lessons 6, 8, 11, 15). 3. Each effect is cheap alone, and frames are shared. The accent word alone made 0 slow frames; on the full page the same word sat in 4-8 per run. Forced layout moved to the next reader. One-term savings were 6-9 ms (E: lessons 1, 6, 30). Designing and reviewing effects one at a time is I. 4. Nobody owns the frame, the threshold or the lifetime. Three effects flipped at 0.72; two drivers shared one property; loops had no visibility owner (E: lessons 4, 14, 18). That this follows from component-per-effect development is I. 5. "Promote it, move it to the GPU" is folklore without a price list. It worked for transforms and opacity, but a compositor blur doubled Composite, layers built at first open cost 6-11 ms, and the final reveal pays 22 MB (E: lessons 13-15). The GPU also slows under heat (E, our setup). 6. The usual metrics are blind where it matters. fps averages, LoAF at 50 ms, scripted scrolls, a CPU throttle that cannot see raster, and no idle phase (E: lessons 23-25). The original team's tooling is I. 7. Desktop-first responsive code. CSS turns effects off on phones while JS keeps driving them, and assets are exported once at the largest size (E: lessons 3, 16; motive I). 8. On our side: confirmation from cheap counts. We predicted from Chrome and Simulator counts, scored the wrong axis, bundled changes, and let probes harden into claims. Each was caught by the phone, the user's thumb, or a source read (E: lessons 24, 27, 28).

5. What to consider when building a site with complex animation and design

Design

  • [ ] Motion inventory. List every moving thing with its trigger (time, scroll position, finger), the property it changes, its cost class per engine (compositor, paint, layout), its visibility condition, its paused frame, its reduced-motion path and its "ended" state (lessons 3, 6, 18).
  • [ ] Budgets per frame and at rest. Main-thread ms, restyled elements, painted Mpx, layers per component, new backing MB, graphics memory, 0 layout reads in moving frames, 0 work at rest (lessons 12, 30).
  • [ ] One owner per scroll threshold. Do not stack a reveal, a theme flip and a nav change at one y (lesson 14).
  • [ ] Design for the fast flick. Reveal tails end before a flick lands. Keep heavy work (layer builds, mounts, decodes) out of the fast start of an eased motion (lessons 13, 14, 22).
  • [ ] Prefer native scroll. Hijacking needs a time-based, compositor-played interaction and finger tests from the first build (lesson 21).
  • [ ] Limit blur. No animated blur on viewport-sized layers; pre-blurred layers cross-fade instead (lesson 15).
  • [ ] Look-alikes are product decisions. For each effect, say whether a pixel-identical compositor form exists and what the fallback look is (lessons 6, 29).

Build

  • [ ] Compositor-only motion. Animate only transform, translate, scale, rotate and opacity, plus filter with care. No var(--x) in keyframes, and no steps() or linear() points on iOS 26 (lessons 2, 5, 6).
  • [ ] Declarative drivers. Scroll position goes to scroll()/view() timelines, time to CSS or animate() with keyword or cubic-bezier easing. Keep a JS fallback behind @supports (lessons 2, 11).
  • [ ] Scroll-timeline traps. Keep the animated set small (WebKit restyles each target every frame), cover the first frame with inline values, use cover 0% cover 100lvh ranges, give both resting ends a bucket, and do not animate backdrop-filter on a scroll timeline (traps after lesson 11).
  • [ ] No per-frame layout reads. Use observers instead of rect tests, and geometry from a ResizeObserver size box. Reads go before writes, once per frame. Remember that scrollY runs pending layout in WebKit (lesson 1).
  • [ ] Animated custom properties stay local. Do not drive them per frame from a section root (lesson 2).
  • [ ] One driver per property. No transition on a property script writes (lesson 4).
  • [ ] Pause per element. Pause off screen, covered and faded states, and resume from held time (lesson 18).
  • [ ] Rest states off the flip. Use opacity: 0.999 and scale(1) rather than 1 and none, and keep elements mounted (lesson 8). Make a no-op state write a real no-op (lesson 3).
  • [ ] Containment that WebKit honours. Definite card sizes from width formulas next to sticky or fixed elements, layout plus size containment, and no size containers around per-frame text (lessons 7, 9).
  • [ ] Layers on a budget. Merge repeated static parts and isolate small ticking ones. Create layers at load or a still moment, never resize them in motion, release them at rest, and write the MB down (lessons 11-13).
  • [ ] Pre-paint reveals. Pre-paint what a reveal will show, and decode images before they appear (lessons 14, 16).
  • [ ] Images at display size. Use srcset/sizes per DPR and modern formats, and flatten decorative stacks into one image (lesson 16).
  • [ ] Timers from the clock. Aim timers at absolute times (lesson 19).
  • [ ] Vendor budget. Third-party tags get a budget and are kept off scroll (lesson 20).
  • [ ] One media query per effect. JS gates effects on exactly the CSS media query, and dead effects are deleted (lesson 3).

Test

  • [ ] A real iPhone with Safari, the slowest target you ship to, from day one, and every engine you ship to (lesson 23).
  • [ ] Counts, not averages. Frames over 34 and 50 ms per zone and interaction, plus the longest frame, logged with scroll position and time since start, with ±4 ms twin thresholds (lessons 25, 28).
  • [ ] Scripted and finger. The user's real gesture, recorded and replayed by a robot, plus a blind finger A/B, plus behaviour checks: settle, momentum, reversal, toolbar (lessons 21, 24).
  • [ ] Heat. Test warm (Serious) as well as cool, and record the thermal state on every run (lessons 19, 26).
  • [ ] Know the limits of Chrome CPU throttling. It slows the main thread only, the factor is calibrated per page and metric, and it is blind to raster, blur, layers and WebKit layout. The Simulator answers function questions, not timing (lesson 23).
  • [ ] A rest test at every scroll position, plus load, first entry, re-entry, first open and later opens (lessons 18, 25).
  • [ ] WebKit Timeline and a layer census (layers, MB, paints) at rest, mid-motion and after (lessons 12, 13).
  • [ ] Ablation before optimisation. Removing a group shows the most any fix of it can gain (lesson 16).

Guardrails

  • [ ] A performance budget in CI. Restyle, layout, paint, layer and memory counts per change, as the cost cards did here, plus a phone check for anything visual or WebKit-specific (lesson 30; running it in CI is our recommendation, not something this study did).
  • [ ] A/B with a noise floor. A/A at the same load, interleaved order, a pre-registered decision rule and n, correction for many metrics, and invalid runs labelled (lessons 26, 28).
  • [ ] Pixel-identical rule for look-alike changes. A fixed tolerance, frozen frames and A/A pairs; anything visible goes to design as a proposal with live A/B pages (lesson 29).
  • [ ] Platform claims carry a status. OBSERVED until a source, doc or bug confirms it, and never passed on as fact (lesson 27).
